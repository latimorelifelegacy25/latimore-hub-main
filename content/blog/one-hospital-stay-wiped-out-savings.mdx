## A Hypothetical Example

*The names and figures below are an illustrative example, not a real client.*

Imagine a couple, Robert and Linda, who had done everything right. Thirty-two years of work between them, a paid-off car, a mortgage with eight years left, and $280,000 in a 401(k) that had taken decades to build. Then Robert had a stroke at 61. He spent 11 days in the hospital, followed by six weeks of inpatient rehabilitation. Out-of-pocket costs, lost wages, and in-home care totaled more than $140,000 over 18 months. The retirement they'd spent 30 years building was cut in half before either of them had collected a single Social Security check.

## The Gap in Most Pre-Retirement Plans

Traditional life insurance pays a death benefit when you die. It does not pay when you have a stroke. It does not pay when you're diagnosed with cancer. It does not pay when you need a heart bypass that takes you out of work for four months.

That gap — the space between "I am alive but incapacitated" and "my money is protected" — is exactly what **living benefits** are designed to close.

## What Are Living Benefits?

Living benefits are riders built into or added to a life insurance policy that allow you to access a portion of your death benefit while you're still alive, under qualifying conditions. The three most common triggers are:

- **Terminal illness:** typically triggered when a physician certifies a life expectancy of 12–24 months or less
- **Chronic illness:** triggered when the insured is unable to perform a specified number of Activities of Daily Living (ADLs) without assistance
- **Critical illness:** triggered by a specific diagnosis — heart attack, stroke, certain cancers, organ failure

If you trigger a living benefit, you receive a lump sum — typically a percentage of your death benefit — that you can use for any purpose. Medical bills. Lost income. Home modifications. No receipts required. No justification. The money is yours to use as your situation demands.

## Why Most People Don't Know This Exists

Living benefits have been part of the insurance market for years. But they're almost never explained to the people who need them most. Many carriers offer living benefit riders, either built in or added on. Availability, cost, and how a qualifying event is defined vary from carrier to carrier, and many group or employer-provided policies don't include them at all.

## What Living Benefits Do Not Do

I want you to hear the limits from me, not find them in the fine print.

- **They reduce the death benefit.** Whatever you take while you're alive comes out of what your family would receive later.
- **The payout may be smaller than you expect.** An accelerated benefit payment may be significantly less than the amount accelerated, because of an actuarial discount, administrative fees, and other deductions.
- **Not every diagnosis qualifies.** Each rider defines its triggers. A condition has to meet the policy's exact definition, and some riders limit the age or health class at which they can be added.
- **They aren't long-term care insurance.** A chronic illness rider can help, but it was not designed to replace a dedicated long-term care plan.
- **They may not be available everywhere.** Riders vary by carrier, product, and state, and some have waiting periods or require certification by a physician.
- **Tax treatment depends on your situation.** Please review it with your tax advisor.

## How Living Benefits Fit With the Rest of Your Plan

Living benefits are one layer, not the whole safety net. A sound plan for a health crisis usually looks at several things together:

1. **Health insurance:** know your deductible and your out-of-pocket maximum.
2. **Disability coverage:** this replaces part of your paycheck. Living benefits don't.
3. **Emergency savings:** the first dollars you'll reach for are the ones in your account.
4. **Life insurance with living benefit riders:** a lump sum that you can use however your situation requires, when a qualifying event happens.

## Questions to Ask About the Coverage You Already Have

Pull out your policy, or call the carrier, and ask:

- Does my policy include accelerated benefit riders for critical, chronic, or terminal illness?
- Is there a cost, or is it included? What is the rider's maximum payout?
- How does the policy define each qualifying condition?
- If I accelerate part of the benefit, what happens to my premiums and my remaining death benefit?
- Are there waiting periods or age limits?

If you can't get clear answers, that tells you something too. A policy review is the quickest way to find out where you stand, before a health event forces the question.
